Dropdown Time Tax: Lifetime Selection Time Estimate

Form DTT-__NUM__ · Art Insights series · Estimated completion time: 3 minutes · * required

Residents whose place of residence appears near the top of an alphabetical dropdown menu (for example, Alaska, the second U.S. state) spend less time selecting it than residents near the bottom (for example, Wyoming). This form measures how fast you select from a dropdown, then estimates the time your position in the alphabet costs you over a lifetime. Complete Step 1 first. Steps 2 to 4 use your measured speed.

Technical notes

1. Purpose and scope

This form estimates the additional time a person spends selecting a place of residence from an alphabetically ordered dropdown menu (an HTML select element) because of that place's position in the list. Only the position-dependent part of each selection is counted. Time spent opening the menu, reading the field label, and confirming the choice is the same for the first and the last entry and is excluded. Estimates are illustrative. They are computed from your own measured selection speed and from published lists and population counts, combined with usage assumptions that are stated in Section 5 and are not drawn from any survey.

2. Measurement of selection speed (Step 1)

The speed test presents 8 target names drawn from the 249-entry country list used in Step 3. Trial j (j = 1, …, 8) draws its target at random from the j-th eighth of the list, so the targets span positions near the top through near the bottom. The timer starts when the target name is displayed and stops when the matching entry is selected; selecting a wrong entry does not stop the timer. For each trial the page records the target position i (counting the first real entry as 1) and the elapsed time t in seconds, measured with the browser's high-resolution clock (performance.now). An ordinary least-squares line t = a + b·i is fitted to the 8 points. The slope b, in seconds per row, is your selection speed; the intercept a absorbs reading the target, opening the menu, and confirming. A negative slope is set to zero. The correlation coefficient r is reported as a check on fit quality. Users who select by typing the first letters of an entry (browser type-ahead) typically produce a slope near zero, which correctly implies that list position costs them almost nothing. If the test is not completed, a default slope of 35 ms per row is used. The default is an estimate derived from an earlier mixed-method model of this series (40% mouse with 20 visible rows and a 12 rows/s wheel scroll, 8% keyboard type-ahead, 37% touch picker at 0.03 s per row, 15% browser autofill), which gave about 1.8 s for the 51st entry of the state list relative to the 1st, i.e. about 36 ms per row.

3. Computation of the per-selection tax

For an entry at position i in a list, the per-selection tax is τ = b·(i − 1) seconds, the predicted extra time relative to the first entry in the same list. For Step 4, the tax is the sum of the country term and the city term, τ = b·(icountry − 1) + b·(icity − 1), because the user must select both. The same measured slope is applied to every list. Lists longer than the test list (for example, the 3,407 U.S. cities in Step 4) are an extrapolation beyond the measured range; see Section 6. Each step also reports the time you actually took for that selection, measured from first pointer or key contact with the menu to the change of value, and the time predicted by your fitted line (a + b·i) for comparison.

4. Computation of lifetime and population totals

Lifetime liability is L = τ · n · Y, where n is the number of selections of that kind per year and Y is the number of form-filling years (Section 5). Annual population liability is P = τ · n · N, where N is the resident population of the selected place. P is summed person-time and is reported in years when large. The comparison figures in each step (Alaska and Wyoming; Afghanistan and Zimbabwe, which are the first and last entries in the country list as sorted here) use the same formula and the same slope.

6. Lists and ordering

Step 2 lists the 50 states and the District of Columbia by full name. Territories are excluded; adding American Samoa would move every entry after "Alaska" down one place but would not change Alaska's position. Step 1 and Step 3 list the 249 entries of ISO 3166-1, using the ISO short name, or the ISO common name where one is defined (for example "Bolivia", "Iran", "Taiwan"). Step 4 lists, for the selected country, every populated place in the GeoNames cities15000 extract (population 15,000 or more). Where two places in the same list share a name, the U.S. state code or the place's population is appended to distinguish them. All lists are sorted with the browser's English collation (Intl.Collator, base sensitivity), so "Åland Islands" sorts with A; another sort order would shift positions slightly. Live sites vary: some place the United States or another country first, some use search boxes or autocomplete, and some sort by postal code (in which case Alaska, AK, is first and Wyoming, WY, is still last). Those designs change the result and are not modelled.

7. Limitations

(a) The linear model assumes each additional row costs the same time. Mouse users scroll only past rows hidden below the open menu (about 20 in Chromium-based browsers on Windows and Linux), so the true cost is lower near the top of a list and higher further down; the fitted slope averages over this. (b) Eight trials give an imprecise slope; a single slow trial can move it noticeably. Repeat the test to check. (c) For lists much longer than 249 entries, people usually switch strategy (type-ahead, browser find, or giving up and typing in a free-text field), so the Step 4 city figures overstate the cost for very long lists. They are the time you would spend if you kept scrolling at your measured rate. (d) Selection counts per year are assumptions, and the lifetime figure is proportional to them. (e) Population figures differ in date and definition between sources (Section 8). (f) Times are reported as continuous clock time: "2 hours" means 2 hours of selecting summed over a lifetime.

8. Data sources

Country population: World Bank, World Development Indicators, indicator SP.POP.TOTL, latest year available (2025), via the datasets/population repository; no figure is published for Taiwan and many small territories, which are shown as "not available". City list and city population: GeoNames (geonames.org), cities15000 extract as distributed in the geonamescache package version __GNC_VER__, licensed CC BY 4.0; GeoNames populations come from mixed census years and should be read as approximate. U.S. life expectancy: National Center for Health Statistics, "Mortality in the United States, 2023", Data Brief No. 521 (December 2024).
